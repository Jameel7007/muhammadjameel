Before tasawwuf had a name, it had a bench. Along the northern wall of the Prophet's mosque in Madinah there was a raised platform of unbaked brick, shaded by palm fronds, and on it lived the companions who owned nothing at all. History remembers them as Ahl al-Suffa, the People of the Bench.[^1]

They were not there by accident. Some had left everything behind in Makkah; some had arrived from distant tribes with nothing but the clothes they stood in. What they shared was a decision, renewed daily, to remain near the Prophet rather than go out and secure their portion of the world. They memorized what he said. They watched how he moved. They were, in the most literal sense, students of presence.

> "Poverty was not their condition. It was their curriculum."

The later masters of the path looked back at the bench the way a river looks back at its spring. Some even traced the word Sufi to Suffa itself, and whether or not the etymology holds, the genealogy does.[^2] Every khanqah, every zawiya, every circle of remembrance held in a borrowed room is a descendant of that platform: a place where people with nothing sat close to everything.

When I first read of the Suffa, I thought of the folding chairs in the back of a mosque in Detroit, and of the brothers who arrived early and left late because the building held something their apartments did not. The furniture changes. The bench does not. In every generation there are people who choose proximity over property, and in every generation the tradition is carried forward not by the comfortable but by them.

The lesson of the bench is not that poverty is holy. It is that nearness has a price, and that the ones who pay it gladly become the ones through whom everyone else receives.

[^1]: Accounts of the Suffa appear throughout the hadith literature; Abu Hurayra, its most famous resident, narrated more traditions than any other companion.

[^2]: Other derivations point to suf, wool, after the rough garments of the early ascetics. The dispute is old and, in the end, beside the point.
